# Research brief: Does Kenya need an Energy Management System?

## Context

The initial source set comprises two Energy and Petroleum Regulatory Authority
(EPRA) reports:

1. *Energy & Petroleum Statistics Report 2026*
2. *Biannual Statistics Report 2025-2026*

Verify the formal titles, publication details, covered periods, and relevant
definitions in the reports before relying on them.

## Research question

Based on the data in these reports, is there a real, evidence-backed need for
an Energy Management System (EMS) in Kenya? If so, at what level and in what
form?

For this study, an EMS means software (with supporting hardware/data
integration) that monitors, forecasts, optimizes, and/or controls energy
generation, storage, and consumption. It may operate at different levels:
national/grid, utility, county, mini-grid, commercial & industrial (C&I),
institutional/public buildings, or household.

## Ground rules

- **Neutrality:** Do not start from the assumption that an EMS is needed. A
  finding of "no clear need", "need exists but an EMS is not the right
  solution", or "insufficient evidence" is as valid as a positive one.
- **Evidence only:** Every finding must cite its source (report, section,
  table, or page where possible). Do not invent figures. If outside knowledge
  is used, label it clearly as **external, unverified**.
- **Separate fact from inference:** Mark each statement as **DATA** (directly
  from the reports), **CALCULATION** (derived; show the working), or
  **INTERPRETATION** (judgment).
- **Transparency:** Flag data gaps, inconsistencies between the reports, and
  changes in definitions or methodology.
- **Diagnosis before solutions:** Do not jump to solutions until the diagnosis
  is complete.

## Method

### Phase 1: Data inventory

List what the two reports cover (generation, demand, peak load,
imports/exports, connections, losses, tariffs, reliability, distributed
generation, mini-grids, fuel consumption, prices, etc.), the periods covered,
and what is notably missing. Note what this data can and cannot reveal about
energy management.

### Phase 2: Broad scan for patterns

Without a preset conclusion, examine the data for trends, anomalies,
imbalances, and inefficiencies, such as:

- Demand growth versus supply and capacity growth
- Peak versus off-peak behavior and load factor
- Generation mix shifts, variability, and curtailment or constraints
- System and distribution losses
- Reliability and quality-of-supply indicators
- Growth in distributed or embedded generation, net metering, and mini-grids
- Consumption patterns by customer category
- Fuel and petroleum dependence, imports, and price movements
- Cost and tariff trends
- Any other pattern found significant

Report each pattern as an observation, not yet a problem.

### Phase 3: Problem and opportunity identification

For each significant observation, assess whether it represents a problem, an
opportunity, or neither. State the magnitude (how big, how many people or how
much money or energy, and the trend).

### Phase 4: Root-cause and solution-fit test

For each problem or opportunity, ask:

- What is the root cause? (Infrastructure, policy, financing, behavior,
  data/visibility, operations, market structure, etc.)
- Is the root cause one that better energy monitoring, forecasting,
  optimization, or control would address?
- Or is it better solved by something else (new capacity, regulation, tariffs,
  financing, hardware upgrades)?
- Is there evidence in the reports that an EMS-type capability already exists
  or is being deployed?

Score the EMS relevance of each item (**High / Medium / Low / None**) with
justification.

### Phase 5: Hypothesis testing

Test at least these competing hypotheses against the evidence:

- **H0:** Kenya's energy challenges do not require an EMS (they are mainly
  supply, infrastructure, financing, or policy issues).
- **H1:** A significant need for EMS exists at one or more levels.
- **H2:** The need exists but is already adequately served or is better
  addressed another way.

Add any other hypothesis suggested by the data. For each, list supporting
evidence, contradicting evidence, and confidence level.

### Phase 6: Verdict

State a clear conclusion: **Need confirmed / Need partially confirmed / Need
not confirmed / Inconclusive**. Give a confidence level and state what
additional data would change the conclusion.

### Phase 7: EMS type and level (only if the verdict is positive or partially positive)

Recommend only the type(s) of EMS most supported by the evidence. Evaluate
each level (grid/utility, county, mini-grid, C&I, institutional/public
buildings, household, other) against:

- Size of the problem or opportunity at that level
- Number of potential users and their ability to adopt
- Data availability and technical feasibility
- Regulatory fit and potential barriers
- Likely impact (energy saved, cost reduced, reliability improved, emissions
  avoided)
- Suitability for an open-source approach, as a separate and clearly labeled
  consideration

Rank the levels and recommend where to start and why.

### Phase 8: Deliverables

Close the study by recommending deliverables that should follow, tailored to
the findings (for example: problem statement, requirements specification,
architecture outline, pilot design, data strategy, stakeholder map, funding or
partnership approach, further research needed). For each, state its purpose,
priority, and the evidence that justifies it.

## Required output format

1. Executive summary (maximum 250 words): question, verdict, confidence, key
   reasons
2. Data inventory and limitations
3. Key observations (table: observation | source | type | magnitude)
4. Problems and opportunities with EMS-relevance scoring
5. Hypothesis testing
6. Verdict and confidence
7. EMS type and level recommendation (only if applicable)
8. Recommended deliverables
9. Data gaps and suggested follow-up research

Write in clear, simple language. Use tables where they aid comparison. End by
stating the single strongest piece of evidence for and against the conclusion.
